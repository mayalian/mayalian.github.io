About

Who I Am

I have always learned by making things.

Drawing came first, not because I wanted to become an artist, but because it was the easiest way to explore ideas that only existed in my imagination.

As my projects became more ambitious, every new challenge demanded a different tool. I learned programming to build an interactive rhythm game. I learned Blender to build places that didn't exist. I discovered Power BI when static presentations could no longer answer the questions I wanted people to ask. Today I'm learning Autodesk Maya to help build NASA's Moon project.

I rarely learn a tool because someone tells me it is useful. I learn new tools when a problem becomes too interesting to solve with the ones I already know.

Although my projects look very different on the surface, from games and scientific visualization to civic analytics and space exploration, they all explore the same question: How can complex systems become easier for people to understand? That question continues to shape everything I build.

Understanding Cities Through Data

CompassPoint Mentorship Program · 2026
Question: Can better visualization change the questions people ask?
Question 04
Through the CompassPoint Mentorship Program, I explored how AI can help compare municipal financial systems that were never designed to be compared.
The Challenge
Each city's financial report runs hundreds of pages, and no two define their categories the same way. My first version was a PowerPoint that could only answer questions I'd already asked. The real engineering challenge wasn't building the dashboard. It was going city by city, deciding what was actually comparable, and building a framework that allowed meaningful comparisons across different accounting systems.
"The dashboard wasn't the breakthrough. Building a common language across different cities was."

Sequential Art · 2025
20 Years Reunion
Question: How do you make silence speak?
Two former friends reunite after twenty years apart. The challenge wasn't writing their reunion, it was communicating it without dialogue. Every panel became an experiment in timing, body language, and emotional pacing, exploring how silence can carry as much weight as words. Drawn in one evening, I left the sketches intentionally rough to preserve the immediacy of the emotion.
Illustration · 2024–2026
CG5 Annual Concert Poster
Question: How do you make a concert poster communicate emotion before someone reads a word?
What began as a self-initiated project grew into designing CG5's concert posters for three consecutive years. A concert poster has only a few seconds to make someone stop scrolling. Before anyone notices the details, they should already feel the energy, tension, or anticipation of the performance. Leading a team of artists, I designed the overall composition, character layouts, lighting, and final rendering, creating most of each poster myself. Drawn during my academically heaviest year, the 2026 poster featured over a dozen individually designed characters, took more than 100 hours to complete, and was personally signed by CG5. Every visual decision served the same goal: helping the audience feel the concert before it even began.
